# Chemical identity and mechanism

## Bottom-line identification

The preferred material in SE 301 717 B is best described as a **sulfone-containing linear poly(hydroxy ether)**, or more specifically a **bisphenol-S poly(hydroxy ether)** when both preferred monomers are used. In later terminology it sits in the phenoxy-resin / high-molecular-weight epoxy family. It is not the same polymer as commercial polysulfone (PSU) or polyethersulfone (PESU), even though all contain diaryl-sulfone motifs.

The preferred feed pair is:

- **4,4'-sulfonyldiphenol**, historically called p,p'-diphenylol sulfone and now commonly called **bisphenol S (BPS)**; and
- its **diglycidyl ether (DGE-BPS)**, a bifunctional diepoxide.

A base generates or transfers phenoxide. Phenoxide attacks an epoxide ring, normally at its less substituted carbon. Ring opening forms an aryl-ether bond and a secondary alkoxide, which is proton-transferred to a secondary alcohol while another phenoxide is generated. Repetition links two difunctional monomers into an almost linear chain.

A generic segment is:

`-[Ar-O-CH2-CH(OH)-CH2-O-Ar']-n`

where `Ar` and/or `Ar'` contains the neutral diaryl-sulfone bridge `Ar-S(=O)2-Ar`.

## Why this is an "epoxy" that remains thermoplastic

In conventional epoxy curing, epoxides react with multifunctional hardeners and form a three-dimensional network. Here, each principal monomer is nominally **difunctional**, and the patent aims for a nearly 1:1 balance of the two complementary functions. The ideal result is therefore a long, linear chain that softens and flows on heating rather than an infinite network.

Each epoxide opening creates a pendant secondary hydroxyl group. That has two consequences:

1. the polymer is polar, adhesive and capable of hydrogen bonding; and
2. it retains latent reactivity. Those hydroxyls can later be crosslinked or derivatized, and residual epoxide can react with them under stronger conditions.

The original patent tries to suppress unwanted side reactions and branching by choosing nonreactive solvents, excluding water and oxygen, controlling monomer purity, and stopping the reaction at the desired viscosity. A later Shell patent explicitly used the same family of linear diphenol-diepoxide units and lightly crosslinked them through the resulting secondary hydroxyl groups. That later development does not prove a concealed intention in 1959, but it validates the platform interpretation.

## The subtle role of the monosodium BPS "catalyst"

The patent states that any dihydric phenol present as a salt must be counted as dihydroxy compound when calculating the molar ratio. This makes the examples more subtle than a casual reading suggests.

Example 1 charges:

- 1.00 mol DGE-BPS;
- 0.97 mol neutral BPS; and
- 0.05 mol monosodium BPS.

On the patent's own accounting, the effective BPS-derived feed is `0.97 + 0.05 = 1.02 mol` per `1.00 mol` diepoxide, at the phenol-rich edge of the preferred range. Example 6 uses the same effective ratio. Example 5 is essentially balanced at about 1.002:1 if its 0.002 mol sodium salt is included.

The salt is catalytic in the sense that phenoxide character can be transferred through proton exchange, but the BPS molecule carrying sodium is itself a difunctional species that can be incorporated. It therefore affects both kinetics and end-group/stoichiometric bookkeeping.

## What can break linearity or cap chains

The ideal AA + BB model is only an approximation. Important deviations include:

- **water and alcohols**, which can open epoxides or create monofunctional ends;
- **hydrolyzable chloride and alpha-glycol impurities** in glycidyl ethers;
- **mono-functional phenols or epoxides**, including impurities;
- **o,p'-BPS isomer**, which changes geometry and may disrupt regularity, packing or effective functionality;
- **epoxide homopolymerization** or alkoxide attack on residual epoxide, which can branch;
- **secondary-hydroxyl/epoxide reaction**, especially with epoxide excess or harsher catalysis;
- **premature crystallization**, which physically removes growing chains from the reactive solution; and
- **thermal chain scission or rearrangement** during high-temperature pressing.

The patent's solvent, dryness, purity and temperature requirements can therefore be read as one integrated strategy: keep both functional groups chemically available and keep the growing chains physically mobile until the desired molecular weight is reached.

## Distinction from PSU and PESU

Commercial aromatic polysulfones are generally made by nucleophilic aromatic substitution between a bisphenolate and an activated aromatic dihalide such as 4,4'-dichlorodiphenyl sulfone. Their backbones are highly aromatic and contain aryl-ether and aryl-sulfone bonds, but no regularly spaced hydroxypropyl segments.

Klootwijk's preferred polymer instead contains:

- aliphatic `-CH2-CH(OH)-CH2-` units created by epoxide opening;
- a secondary hydroxyl at every ring-opening event;
- potentially greater water affinity and chemical functionalizability; and
- a lower-temperature, more chemically reactive route than a fully aromatic SNAr polymerization.

Calling the material simply "polysulfone" would therefore erase the feature that is most technologically interesting: it is a **reactive engineering thermoplastic** rather than only a heat-resistant fully aromatic thermoplastic.
