# Executive summary - what Arie Klootwijk's patent was really doing

## Finding in one sentence

Arie Klootwijk and his co-inventors were not disguising an SO2-release or biological-sulfur invention; they were engineering an early sulfone-enhanced **linear poly(hydroxy ether)** whose molecular weight, phase behaviour, processability and latent reactivity were deliberately controlled as one integrated system.

## 1. Chemical identity

The `SO2` unit is a neutral sulfone bridge between two aromatic rings:

`Ar-S(=O)2-Ar`

It is not free sulfur dioxide, a sulfite, a sulfonate, a sulfonyl chloride or an ionic site. In the preferred system, the sulfone appears in bisphenol S (BPS), its diglycidyl ether (DGE-BPS), or both.

Phenoxide attacks an epoxide carbon, opening the epoxide ring. Every coupling event forms:

- one new ether bond in the main chain; and
- one secondary hydroxyl group on the hydroxypropyl segment.

Because the dihydroxy and diepoxide components are each bifunctional, the ideal product is predominantly linear. That is why the material remains thermoplastic instead of becoming the familiar three-dimensional cured epoxy network.

The most accurate modern family description is a **sulfone-containing poly(hydroxy ether)** or high-molecular-weight/phenoxy-type epoxy thermoplastic. It is chemically distinct from commercial fully aromatic PSU/PESU, which is usually made by nucleophilic aromatic substitution and lacks the same hydroxypropyl repeat structure.

## 2. Why near-equimolarity matters

For an ideal bifunctional step-growth polymerisation, molecular size depends sharply on both conversion and functional-group balance. With `r` defined as the smaller functional-group population divided by the larger:

`Xn = (1+r)/(1+r-2rp)`

At complete limiting-group conversion:

`Xn,max = (1+r)/(1-r)`

This creates a hard stoichiometric ceiling. Illustratively:

- `r = 0.98` gives `Xn,max about 99`;
- `r = 0.97` gives `Xn,max about 65.7`;
- `r = 0.96` gives `Xn,max about 49`; and
- `r = 0.94` gives `Xn,max about 32.3`.

Thus a feed described in ordinary patent language as “substantially equimolar” is not vague decoration. A one-to-four-percent imbalance can determine whether the product behaves as a brittle oligomer, a useful entangled thermoplastic or an unmanageably high-molecular-weight material.

The narrow ratio also selects end-group chemistry. A slight phenol excess suppresses residual epoxide end groups and can terminate growth deliberately, while a slight epoxide excess does the reverse.

## 3. The overlooked catalyst bookkeeping

In Swedish Example 1, the nominal feed is 1.00 mol DGE-BPS and 0.97 mol neutral BPS, with 0.05 mol monosodium BPS used as catalyst.

The patent expressly says that phenolic material present as its salt is included in the ratio. On that basis, the effective BPS-derived molecular feed is:

`0.97 + 0.05 = 1.02 mol BPS-derived molecules per 1.00 mol DGE-BPS`

That places the experiment at the phenol-rich edge of the preferred window. The catalyst therefore does two jobs:

1. it generates the reactive phenoxide and controls kinetics; and
2. it participates in the functional-group and end-group balance.

This is not evidence of a code or concealment. It is evidence of precise polymer design.

## 4. What was technically advanced

The patent treats several variables that are often separated in a superficial reading as one coupled process window:

- monomer functionality and equivalent balance;
- purity and isomer control;
- water exclusion;
- catalyst identity and loading;
- solvent dielectric character and chemical inertness;
- polymer solubility during the last increments of conversion;
- crystallisation/precipitation timing;
- reaction temperature and residence time; and
- deliberate chain stopping.

The central process problem was not merely “make an epoxy reaction occur.” It was to keep a rapidly growing, increasingly rigid and polar chain mobile and soluble long enough to reach the molecular-weight region where toughness and engineering performance emerge.

The invention therefore combines reaction chemistry with phase and transport control. Premature crystallisation or precipitation stops effective chain growth; the selected polar, nonreactive media delay that event. The final material may still crystallise after isolation, so crystallinity is not rejected - its timing is controlled.

## 5. What the historical data imply

The Swedish examples report an all-sulfone material with intrinsic viscosity 0.43, yield stress about 770 kgf/cm2 (about 75.5 MPa), impact strength 650 kg.cm/cm2 and heat-distortion temperature 154 C. The crystalline product was reported to melt near 230 C.

Examples 2 and 3 are particularly revealing. At intrinsic viscosity 0.35, impact strength was only about 110 kg.cm/cm2. At intrinsic viscosities 0.46 and 0.50, impact strength rose to about 650 and 620 kg.cm/cm2. The sparse historical data therefore suggest an entanglement/molecular-weight threshold rather than a smooth linear improvement.

The U.S. counterpart contains a comparison series in which increasing sulfone content tracks with higher yield stress, impact and Vicat softening temperature. The comparison is directionally strong but not perfectly isolated because intrinsic viscosity also rises. A modern study must match absolute molecular weight and thermal history before assigning the entire improvement to the sulfone bridge.

## 6. What the pendant hydroxyls enable

The secondary OH groups are the feature that makes the platform broader than a conventional inert moulding plastic. They can provide:

- strong adhesion to polar substrates;
- hydrogen bonding and cohesive strength;
- miscibility or specific interactions with compatible polymers;
- wetting and coupling at fibre or filler surfaces;
- crosslinking through isocyanates, melamine/formaldehyde, phenolic systems or other OH-reactive chemistry;
- covalent attachment of functional side groups; and
- reactive participation at epoxy/thermoset interfaces.

The polymer can therefore occupy a useful middle ground: processable as a thermoplastic, yet chemically addressable after shaping.

## 7. Evidence-weighted modern uses

### Highest confidence

- PCB build-up, dielectric and adhesive films, after dielectric, ionic-purity, thermal-cycling and extractables qualification.
- Coatings, primers and hot-melt/reactive adhesives.
- Thermoplastic toughener or reactive interlayer in epoxy and composite systems.
- Moulded, extruded or fibre products where higher heat performance than conventional BPA phenoxy is valuable.

### Medium to medium-high confidence

- Corrosion-resistant binders and barrier or tie-layer films.
- Blends with PES/PESU or other hydrogen-bond-accepting polymers, including membrane-support or membrane-modifier research.

### Research hypotheses only

- Reactive additive-manufacturing feedstock.
- Biosensor, microfluidic or bio-device surface layers where the hydroxyls are derivatised and extractables are controlled.

### Unsupported or presently low confidence

- SO2 storage or release.
- Intrinsic ion- or proton-exchange membranes without introducing ionic groups.
- Direct implant or tissue-scaffold use without a complete modern toxicology and extractables programme.

## 8. Bioengineering interpretation

From a biological-engineering viewpoint, the most interesting feature is not sulfur signalling. It is the combination of a mechanically robust aromatic thermoplastic backbone with a dense, regular population of chemically accessible hydroxyls.

That could be valuable in non-implant bioengineering contexts such as microfluidic bonding, immobilisation layers, diagnostic-device coatings, antifouling grafting, membrane support or controlled surface functionalisation. None of those uses is validated by the historical patent. They require assessment of:

- residual BPS and DGE-BPS;
- oligomer distribution and migration;
- hydrolytic and oxidative aging;
- sterilisation effects;
- surface chemistry stability;
- cytotoxicity, sensitisation and irritation; and
- application-specific leachables.

Current BPS concern makes the distinction between high-molecular-weight polymer and mobile low-molecular-weight species crucial, not optional.

## 9. Best modern validation experiment

A compact, information-rich programme would prepare matched-molecular-weight versions of:

1. BPA/DGEBA phenoxy;
2. BPS/DGEBA mixed architecture;
3. BPS/DGE-BPS all-sulfone architecture; and
4. a deliberately phenol-ended BPS/DGE-BPS material.

At matched molecular weight and thermal history, compare:

- SEC with suitable calibration or absolute detection;
- quantitative NMR and end-group analysis;
- DSC, DMA, TGA and melt rheology;
- crystallisation kinetics and wide-angle X-ray scattering;
- tensile, impact, fracture and aging performance;
- adhesion and interfacial fracture energy;
- dielectric constant, loss and breakdown strength;
- water uptake, dimensional stability and barrier properties;
- residual monomers/oligomers by validated LC-MS/GC-MS methods; and
- crosslinking or derivatisation through the pendant hydroxyls.

The first decisive question is whether the sulfone architecture retains a property advantage when molecular weight, topology and thermal history are controlled. The second is whether the pendant-OH chemistry produces exceptional adhesion or thermoset compatibility without unacceptable moisture uptake or extractables.

## 10. Final judgement

The most compelling reading is not that Arie Klootwijk hid a different invention under practical examples. It is that the patent's apparently ordinary process language already contained the real revolution:

> A sulfone-enhanced phenoxy platform designed to be processed as a thermoplastic, while preserving enough built-in hydroxyl chemistry to function as an adhesive, reactive film, composite modifier or chemically addressable interface.

That is a sophisticated and historically early materials concept. The package's technical review explains the evidence, the limitations and a path for testing the idea with modern instrumentation.
