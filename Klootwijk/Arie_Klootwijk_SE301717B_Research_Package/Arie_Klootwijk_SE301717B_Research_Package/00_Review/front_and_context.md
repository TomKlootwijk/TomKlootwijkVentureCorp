# Executive finding

\begin{verdictbox}
\textbf{The most defensible conclusion is not that the patent concealed a different SO$_2$ technology. It is that it disclosed, unusually early, a reactive high-performance thermoplastic platform whose significance is easy to miss if it is read only as a fibre-and-moulding patent.}

The diaryl-SO$_2$ unit is a neutral sulfone bridge in the polymer backbone, not sulfur-dioxide gas, a sulfonate, a biological signalling group or an ion-exchange site. The near-equimolar language is the ordinary but unforgiving mathematics of bifunctional step-growth polymerisation: a 1-4\% imbalance can impose a hard ceiling on chain length. The genuinely forward-looking part is the integration of stoichiometry, monomer purity, dryness, solvent polarity, solubility and phase control to produce a \emph{linear epoxy-derived polymer} that remains thermoplastic and carries a secondary hydroxyl at every ring-opening event.
\end{verdictbox}

The patent's preferred reaction - bisphenol S (BPS) plus its diglycidyl ether - produces what would now be recognised as a **sulfone-containing poly(hydroxy ether)**, related to phenoxy or high-molecular-weight epoxy resins. It is distinct from commercial fully aromatic polysulfone/polyethersulfone, because its chain contains hydroxypropyl segments and abundant pendant OH groups.

Those hydroxyls make the material more than an inert moulding resin. They provide adhesion, hydrogen bonding, blend compatibility and a route to later crosslinking or derivatisation. A later Shell patent explicitly used linear diphenol-diepoxide units and lightly crosslinked them through the resulting secondary hydroxyl groups. A later BPS high-molecular-weight epoxy patent applied closely related ratio, purity and molecular-weight logic to printed-circuit-board insulating films. These are strong signs of technological lineage, though not proof that a single commercial product ran continuously from the 1959 work.

## Answer to the "what was he up to?" question

Arie Klootwijk and his co-inventors appear to have been doing four things at once:

1. **Turning epoxy chemistry into a linear engineering thermoplastic rather than a cured three-dimensional network.**
2. **Using the aryl-sulfone bridge to raise softening, heat-distortion and mechanical performance.**
3. **Solving a process-physics problem:** growing chains precipitated or crystallised too early, so the team selected polar, nonreactive solvents that kept the product mobile until high molecular weight was reached.
4. **Programming chain length and end groups** with near-equivalent bifunctional feeds, anhydrous phenolate catalyst and deliberate phenol-rich stopping.

That combination was genuinely sophisticated for a 1959 priority filing. It anticipated themes that are now familiar in high-performance polymers: reactive thermoplastics, thermoplastic-to-thermoset conversion, toughening of epoxy matrices, high-temperature dielectric films and structure-property tuning by polar aromatic groups.

## What the evidence does *not* support

There is no documentary or chemical evidence that the patent was a disguised route to:

- controlled release or storage of SO$_2$;
- intrinsic proton or ion transport;
- biological sulfur signalling;
- drug delivery, tissue scaffolds or implants; or
- a hidden energetic or catalytic material unrelated to polymers.

Those hypotheses fail because the sulfur is locked in a stable neutral diaryl sulfone and every claim, example, property test and later citation stays within macromolecular materials technology.

## Best modern application bets

The strongest opportunities, ranked by evidence, are:

- high-temperature dielectric, adhesive and build-up films for electronics;
- coatings, primers, hot-melt or reactive adhesives;
- thermoplastic tougheners and reactive interlayers in epoxy/composite systems;
- moulded, extruded or fibre products where a higher thermal window than BPA phenoxy is valuable;
- corrosion-resistant and barrier layers, subject to moisture and extractables testing; and
- membrane-blend or bio-device components as research hypotheses, not qualified uses.

Direct biological contact is a high-barrier proposition. Current hazard concern around BPS makes residual monomer, DGE-BPS, low oligomers and degradation products central. A high-molecular-weight polymer is not equivalent to free BPS, but that distinction must be demonstrated analytically and toxicologically.

## The most revealing overlooked detail

Example 1 is often read as 0.97 mol BPS against 1.00 mol DGE-BPS. The patent says phenol present as salt is included in the ratio. Adding the 0.05 mol monosodium-BPS catalyst gives an effective BPS-derived feed of **1.02:1** - exactly the phenol-rich boundary of the preferred range. The catalyst is therefore also part of the functional-group bookkeeping. This is not an encoded secret, but it is a subtle, deliberate piece of molecular-weight and end-group design.

# Scope, method and confidence language

This review uses the user-supplied Swedish patent and its clean English translation as the primary basis. The British family publication and U.S. counterpart are used to resolve terminology and add directly corresponding comparison data. Later patents and literature are clearly labelled as context or extrapolation.

The report uses the following confidence language:

- **High:** directly stated/demonstrated in the original family or strongly validated by a later patent using substantially the same material logic.
- **Medium:** supported by close phenoxy/BPS analogues and by chemical mechanism, but not demonstrated for the exact Klootwijk polymer.
- **Low:** plausible only after substantial new chemistry or qualification.
- **Unsupported:** inconsistent with the claimed structure or lacking any documentary basis.

Historical numerical data are preserved in their published units; yield stress is also converted to MPa. No attempt is made to convert intrinsic viscosity into absolute molecular weight without a polymer-specific Mark-Houwink relation.

## Key limitations

The patent provides single historical examples, not a modern replicated dataset. It lacks SEC/GPC, molecular-weight distribution, spectroscopic structure confirmation, branching analysis, statistical uncertainty, long-term creep/fatigue, dielectric testing, flame testing, extractables/leachables and biocompatibility. The modern application discussion is therefore an evidence-weighted research roadmap, not a product qualification.

# What the patent literally covers

## Identity, dates and inventors

SE 301 717 B names R. W. Kreps, Arie Klootwijk and J. M. Goppel as inventors and Shell Internationale Research Maatschappij N.V. as applicant. The priority is Netherlands application 240 201 of 15 June 1959. The Swedish application was filed on 15 June 1960 and the laid-open specification was published on 17 June 1968. The British family publication appeared in 1963, and the corresponding U.S. continuation was issued in January 1968.

The Swedish title can read as though the diepoxide or both compounds must contain SO$_2$, but the body and Claim 1 are broader: the dihydroxy compound, the diepoxide, or both may contain the diaryl-sulfone bridge.

## Claim 1 as a process-window claim

The core claim requires the combination of:

- one or more dihydroxy compounds;
- a substantially equimolar quantity of one or more diepoxy compounds;
- an aryl-SO$_2$-aryl group in either or both reactant families;
- an alkaline catalyst;
- a substantially anhydrous ketone, nitrile, nitro compound, sulfoxide or sulfone solvent;
- solvent dielectric constant at least 5;
- solubility of both reactants and the growing polymer;
- low solvent reactivity toward phenol and epoxide; and
- continuation until intrinsic viscosity reaches at least 0.4.

This is not a claim to the sulfone group in isolation. It claims a coupled chemical and physical process that prevents premature termination.

The claim ladder reveals the inventors' hierarchy: first secure the homogeneous high-conversion process, then narrow to geometry, molecular weight, polarity, dryness, catalyst integration and monomer purity.

## The problem they said they solved

Earlier art allowed diphenylol sulfone and glycidyl chemistry but used ethanol or dioxane. The inventors attributed inferior products to either:

- crystallisation/precipitation of the product before sufficient molecular weight was reached; or
- solvent acting as a chain stopper.

Their solution was to keep the growing macromolecule in a solvent that dissolved it and did not attack either functional group. This is a phase-behaviour intervention as much as a reaction-chemistry intervention.
