# What the SO2 bridge does - and does not do

## It is a diaryl sulfone, not "SO2 gas in a polymer"

The structural element is `Ar-S(=O)2-Ar`: sulfur in a high oxidation state, doubly bonded to two oxygens and covalently bound to two aromatic rings. It is:

- neutral rather than ionic;
- a backbone bridge rather than a pendant acid;
- strongly polar;
- relatively rigid and bulky; and
- electronically withdrawing from the aromatic rings.

It is not `SO2` dissolved in the material, not a sulfite, not a sulfonate and not a sulfonyl chloride. Under intended use it is not expected to liberate sulfur dioxide as a controlled function. Any sulfur-containing products of destructive pyrolysis would be decomposition products, not the claimed technology.

## Structure-property role

The most defensible molecular effects are:

- **higher cohesive energy and dipole-dipole interaction**, raising resistance to segmental motion;
- **greater backbone rigidity** than an isopropylidene or simple alkylene bridge;
- **higher softening/heat-distortion response**, directly shown in the U.S. comparison;
- **altered crystallisation and packing**, seen in the crystalline all-sulfone product;
- **enhanced polarity and solvent interactions**; and
- **increased phenol acidity**, which makes phenolate generation easier, while the electron-withdrawing ring can also moderate nucleophilicity.

The original U.S. counterpart makes the comparison unusually explicit. In its first three polymers, sulfone-bearing feed components are increased from zero to one to two:

| Polymer | Feed pair | Intrinsic viscosity | Yield stress | Tensile impact* | Vicat softening |
|---:|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | BPA + DGEBA | 0.57 | 600 kgf/cm2 (58.8 MPa) | 265 | 103 C |
| 2 | BPS + DGEBA | 0.67 | 750 kgf/cm2 (73.5 MPa) | 500 | 135 C |
| 3 | BPS + DGE-BPS | 0.70 | 820 kgf/cm2 (80.4 MPa) | 770 | 175 C |

The trend is compelling, but not perfectly isolated: intrinsic viscosity also rises across the series. A modern experiment should compare matched absolute molecular weights and identical thermal histories.

## Polarity is beneficial and costly

The sulfone plus regularly spaced secondary OH groups gives strong polar interactions and potential adhesion. It also increases water affinity. In the Swedish all-sulfone Example 1, mass gain after 14 days at 20 C reaches 5.6 wt% in water, 5.1 wt% in 5% NaOH and 5.4 wt% in 5% sulfuric acid. The patent says no chemical change occurred, but modern use would require dimensional, mechanical, dielectric and extractables retention after conditioning.

## Why it is not intrinsically ion-conducting

An ion-exchange polymer needs fixed ionic groups such as sulfonate, phosphonate or quaternary ammonium. A neutral diaryl sulfone provides dipolar character but no dissociable charge. Post-sulfonation could create an ionomer, but that would be a new material with different water uptake, morphology and hydrolytic behaviour.

# Experimental evidence in the Swedish examples

## Example map

| Example | Core architecture | Process | IV in DMF | Selected outcome |
|---|---|---|---:|---|
| 1 | DGE-BPS + BPS | acetophenone, 100 C, 60 h | 0.43 | yield 75.5 MPa; impact 650; HDT 154 C; crystalline melt about 230 C |
| 2 | DGE-BPS + BPS | same, 20 h | 0.35 | impact only 110 despite similar yield |
| 3 | DGE-BPS + BPS | reheated to 150 C to redissolve | 0.46 / 0.50 | impact 650 / 620 |
| 4 | DGE-BPS + BPS | 150 C, 50 min; BPS stop | 1.37, then 1.29 after pressing | impact 690; very high IV reached rapidly |
| 5 | DGE-BPS + BPS | sulfolane, 105 C, 24 h | 0.42 | alternative polar sulfone solvent works |
| 6 | DGEBA + BPS | Example-4 type route | 0.82 | HDT 114 C; Tg about 130 C; water uptake 1.8% |

## Molecular-weight threshold and toughness

The patent's clearest causal result is the contrast between Example 2 and Examples 1/3. At IV 0.35, tensile impact is 110 historical units. At IV 0.43-0.50, it is roughly 620-650. Yield stress changes much less. The inventors concluded that good impact strength required intrinsic viscosity above 0.35.

\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.86\textwidth]{../05_Figures/figure_05_intrinsic_viscosity_impact.png}
\end{center}

This is exactly what a linear thermoplastic would be expected to show: once chains are long enough for entanglement and effective stress transfer, toughness rises nonlinearly. The data are sparse and unreplicated, but the qualitative threshold is strong.

## The crystallisation paradox

Crystallisation appears in two roles:

1. **A reaction failure mode:** if polymer crystallises out too early, active chain ends lose mobility and molecular weight stalls.
2. **A final material feature:** Example 1 is crystalline and birefringent, with a sharp diffraction pattern and crystallite melting near 230 C.

The invention therefore does not eliminate crystallinity. It delays phase separation until chain growth is sufficient. Example 3 makes the concept operational: temperature is raised to 150 C specifically to redissolve the crystallising product and continue polymerisation.

## Thermal aging at 160 C

Example 1 yield stress rises from 770 to 1,000 kgf/cm2 after 24 hours at 160 C and stays near that level through 100 hours. Possible explanations include annealing, increased crystallinity, physical aging or post-reaction. The result cannot be called unambiguously beneficial because post-aging elongation and impact were not reported. A stiffer/stronger but more brittle material could produce the same yield-stress trend.

## Melt-processing warning at 290 C

Example 4 reaches IV 1.37 but falls to 1.29 after pressing at 290 C, about a 5.8% decrease. Measurement scatter is unknown, so chain scission cannot be proven. Nevertheless, it signals that a modern process window must map molecular-weight retention versus temperature and residence time.

## Architecture tuning in Example 6

Replacing DGE-BPS with DGEBA leaves sulfone only in BPS. The product has Tg about 130 C and HDT 114 C, both below the all-sulfone result, but water uptake is only 1.8% after 140 hours. The comparison shows that sulfone content and hydroxyether density can tune heat/water trade-offs rather than produce one fixed material.

# Was the ordinary application language camouflage?

## Why patents sound broader and more mundane than the chemistry

Patents commonly list familiar forms - fibres, films, mouldings, castings, extrusions - to demonstrate utility and obtain broad practical coverage. Such wording can make the underlying materials concept seem less ambitious. Here, however, the claims themselves are technically specific and transparent. They focus on:

- equivalent balance;
- solvent polarity and nonreactivity;
- homogeneous chain growth;
- dryness;
- phenolate catalyst;
- molecular-weight threshold; and
- monomer purity.

That is not the pattern of a document hiding a different mechanism. It is the pattern of a process patent written to protect a difficult polymerisation window.

## The genuinely revolutionary layer

The forward-looking idea is **process now, react later**:

- Build a high-molecular-weight linear chain with epoxy chemistry.
- Retain a secondary OH at every ring opening.
- Shape it as a thermoplastic.
- Optionally exploit the OH functionality later for adhesion, crosslinking or interfacial coupling.

The original patent does not fully develop the fourth step, but the chemistry makes it possible. The later Shell thermoplastic/thermoset patent makes this latent capability explicit. Thus the "hidden" element is better described as an **under-emphasised platform property**, not an obfuscated alternate invention.

## Comparison with a conventional epoxy thermoset

| Feature | Klootwijk linear poly(hydroxy ether) | Conventional multifunctional epoxy cure |
|---|---|---|
| Principal functionality | two + two | often more than two effective network functions |
| Target topology | long, almost-linear chain | 3D network |
| Processing | solution or melt thermoplastic | liquid/low-MW resin then irreversible cure |
| Reworkability | potentially melt/re-dissolve before crosslinking | generally not melt-reprocessable |
| OH generation | one secondary OH per epoxide opening | also generated, but embedded in network |
| Latent reactivity | high; can be post-crosslinked | cure consumes the process window |
| Key risk | stoichiometric imbalance and chain stopping | gelation, cure shrinkage, exotherm |

## 1959-1968: the Klootwijk family of disclosures

The Netherlands priority was filed in 1959. GB 915,767 A published in 1963 and gives an English-language family description. SE 301 717 B and US 3,364,178 A were published in 1968. The U.S. document is a continuation and focuses more explicitly on the thermoplastic poly(hydroxyalkyl diphenyl sulfone) product and the sulfone-content comparison.

## Relation to fully aromatic polysulfone/polyethersulfone

A later dominant route to high-performance sulfone thermoplastics uses a bisphenolate plus activated diaryl dihalide in a nucleophilic aromatic substitution. US 4,108,837 describes high-molecular-weight polyarylene polyethers made in sulfoxide/sulfone solvents under dry, near-equimolar conditions. It states that even a 5% variation from equal molar amounts seriously reduces molecular weight and that polymer remaining dissolved gives higher molecular weight.

That later route independently confirms the same process logic but yields a different backbone: fully aromatic aryl ether-sulfone without the hydroxypropyl units. It helps explain why the Klootwijk material should not simply be called PSU or PESU.

## 1987: latent OH reactivity becomes explicit

EP 0 249 262 A2 describes linear diphenolic/diepoxide units that are lightly crosslinked through the secondary hydroxyls generated during formation. Its goal is a thermoplastic composition with thermosetting processing characteristics and a modulus/Tg/toughness balance. The Klootwijk family is recorded among the cited prior art.

This is the strongest evidence that the original linear poly(hydroxy ether) was a platform capable of being pushed toward thermoplastic-thermoset hybrids.

## 1991: phenoxy in filtration membranes

US 5,076,935 uses a phenoxy resin blended with polyethersulfone in porous filtration membranes. It describes uses including sterile vent filtration for fermentation tanks and reports improved membrane strength and easier melt sealing. The exact BPS poly(hydroxy ether) is not the disclosed phenoxy, so this is a close-analogue application, not direct proof.

## 2002: BPS high-molecular-weight epoxy for PCB film

US 6,451,878 B1 claims high-molecular-weight epoxy resin made from a difunctional epoxy and a dihydric phenol containing 70-100% BPS. It specifies:

- weight-average molecular weight 10,000-200,000;
- epoxy:phenolic-OH equivalent ratio 1:0.94-1.06;
- high 4,4'-BPS purity;
- low hydrolyzable chlorine/alpha-glycol impurity;
- low catalyst-derived ionic contamination; and
- use in printed-circuit-board insulating film.

It reports Tg up to about 155 C and thermal expansion around 50-60 ppm/C versus about 70 ppm/C for conventional epoxy. The echoes of the 1959 logic - equivalent balance, BPS purity, glycidyl impurity, high molecular weight and film processing - are unmistakable.

## 2004: material-class confirmation

Lü, Zheng and Tian published a material explicitly named **poly(hydroxyether sulfone)** synthesised from BPS and epichlorohydrin. They characterised hydrogen-bonding interactions involving OH, sulfone and ether sites. Their route and research question differ from the patent, but the paper confirms that the material identity inferred here is not a retrospective invention.

## Why the Klootwijk route may not have become the dominant "polysulfone"

The historical record in this package does not establish commercial decisions. Chemically plausible reasons include:

- demanding purity and equivalent control;
- solvent-heavy polymerisation and isolation;
- strong crystallisation/phase sensitivity;
- pendant-OH water uptake;
- a narrower melt-stability window than fully aromatic PSU/PESU;
- high-melting DGE-BPS monomer; and
- later availability of fully aromatic sulfone polymers with exceptional continuous-use performance.

These are inferences to test, not documented reasons for abandonment.
